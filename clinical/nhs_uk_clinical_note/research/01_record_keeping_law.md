## 1. GMC — Good Medical Practice (2024)

- **URL:** https://www.gmc-uk.org/professional-standards/the-professional-standards/good-medical-practice
- **PDF:** https://www.gmc-uk.org/cdn/documents/good-medical-practice-2024---english_pdf-102607294.pdf
- **Type:** GMC guidance (professional standards — binding on all GMC registrants)
- **In effect from:** 30 January 2024 (replaces the 2013 edition)

### Key provisions — Domain 3, "Colleagues, culture and safety", section "Recording your work clearly, accurately, and legibly"

The record-keeping requirements are at **paragraphs 69–71** of the 2024 edition. The section heading is part of Domain 3 (Colleagues, culture and safety), which covers paragraphs 48–80.

**Paragraph 69 (exact wording):**
> "You must make sure that formal records of your work (including patients' records) are clear, accurate, contemporaneous⁶ and legible."

Footnote 6: "Contemporaneous means making records at the same time as the events you are recording, or as soon as possible afterwards."

**Paragraph 70 (exact wording):**
> "You should take a proportionate approach to the level of detail but patients' records should usually include:
> a. relevant clinical findings
> b. drugs, investigations or treatments proposed, provided or prescribed
> c. the information shared with patients
> d. concerns or preferences expressed by the patient that might be relevant to their ongoing care, and whether these were addressed
> e. information about any reasonable adjustments and communication support preferences
> f. decisions made, actions agreed (including decisions to take no action) and when/whether decisions should be reviewed
> g. who is creating the record and when."

**Paragraph 71 (exact wording):**
> "You must keep records that contain personal information about patients, colleagues or others securely, and in line with any data protection law requirements and you must follow our guidance on Confidentiality: good practice in handling patient information."

### Changes from the 2013 edition (notable)

Per the GMC's own comparison document (https://www.gmc-uk.org/cdn/documents/good-medical-practice-2024---what-s-new-in-each-domain_pdf-104585778.pdf):

- **Para 69** (2024) replaces **para 19** (2013): "Documents you make (including clinical records) to formally record your work must be clear, accurate and legible." The 2024 version adds the word "contemporaneous" with a pop-up definition.
- **Para 70** (2024) replaces **para 21** (2013): Expanded to include patient preferences/concerns (d), reasonable adjustments and communication support preferences (e), and a proportionality principle. The 2013 version had only 5 sub-paragraphs (a–e); the 2024 version has 7 (a–g).
- **Para 71** (2024) replaces **para 20** (2013): Added explicit reference to GMC's Confidentiality guidance: "and you must follow our guidance on Confidentiality: good practice in handling patient information."

### Domain 4 — "Trust and professionalism" (paras 81–101)

Domain 4 includes:
- **Para 81** (general duties of registered medical professionals, including "Protect patients' personal information from improper disclosure")
- **Para 97** (cooperating with legal and regulatory requirements)

### What it requires of doctors

Doctors must keep clear, accurate, contemporaneous, and legible records containing specific clinical information (findings, treatments, decisions, patient preferences, who recorded and when). Records must be kept securely and in compliance with data protection law. These are professional standards — failure to meet them can put a doctor's registration at risk.

"Good Medical Practice in Action" is the GMC's interactive learning website featuring case-based scenarios that illustrate how GMC guidance applies to real situations. It was described in the BMJ as "interactive case studies" covering topics such as whistleblowing, drug addiction, and child protection. ## 3. Common Law Duty of Confidentiality & Confidentiality: NHS Code of Practice (2003)

### 3a. Common Law Duty of Confidentiality

- **URL (DHSC/Northern Ireland summary):** https://www.health-ni.gov.uk/articles/common-law-duty-confidentiality
- **URL (NHS England):** https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/consent-to-using-and-sharing-patient-information/
- **Type:** Common law (judge-made/case law) — NOT a statute

#### Key provisions (from the DHSC summary):

> "Common law is not written out in one document like an Act of Parliament. It is a form of law based on previous court cases decided by judges."

> "The general position is that if information is given in circumstances where it is expected that a duty of confidence applies, that information cannot normally be disclosed without the information provider's consent."

> "In practice, this means that all patient/client information, whether held on paper, computer, visually or audio recorded, or held in the memory of the professional, must not normally be disclosed without the consent of the patient/client. It is irrelevant for example how old the patient/client is, or what the state of his/her mental health is; the duty still applies."

**Three circumstances making disclosure lawful:**
1. "where the individual to whom the information relates has consented"
2. "where disclosure is necessary to safeguard the individual, or others, or is in the public interest"
3. "where there is a legal duty to do so, for example a court order"

> "If a disclosure is made which is not permitted under common law the patient/client could possibly bring a legal action not only against the organisation but also against the individual responsible for the breach."

The common law duty of confidentiality **extends beyond death** (per the Information Tribunal in Bluck v IC and Epsom & St Helier NHS Trust, EA/2006/0010, 17 Sep 2007; and Lewis v Secretary of State for Health [2008] EWHC 2196).

### 3b. Confidentiality: NHS Code of Practice (2003)

- **URL:** https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/confidentiality-nhs-code-of-practice
- **PDF:** https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7c13f0ed915d210ade16fb/Confidentiality_-_NHS_Code_of_Practice.pdf
- **Supplementary guidance (public interest disclosures):** https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a757267ed915d6faf2b30d6/Confidentiality_-_NHS_Code_of_Practice_Supplementary_Guidance_on_Public_Interest_Disclosures.pdf
- **Type:** NHS code of practice (guidance, not statute)
- **Published:** 7 November 2003, by the Department of Health

#### Key provisions

> "This code of practice sets out standards to ensure that patient information is handled fairly, lawfully and as transparently as possible."

The Code offers detailed guidance on:
- Protecting confidential information
- Informing service users about uses of their personal information
- Offering service users appropriate choices about the uses of their personal information
- The circumstances in which confidential information may be used or disclosed

**Note on currency:** The 2003 Code of Practice predates the Data Protection Act 2018 and UK GDPR. It remains published on gov.uk but has not been formally updated. The GMC's 2017 Confidentiality guidance (updated 2024) and NHS England's "A guide to confidentiality in health and social care" (digital.nhs.uk) now provide more current guidance on the same topic.

### 3c. GMC Confidentiality: good practice in handling patient information (2017, updated 2024)

- **URL:** https://www.gmc-uk.org/professional-standards/the-professional-standards/confidentiality
- **PDF:** https://www.gmc-uk.org/cdn/documents/gmc-guidance-for-doctors---confidentiality-good-practice-in-handling-patient-information----70080105.pdf
- **Type:** GMC guidance (professional standards)
- **In effect from:** 25 April 2017
- **Updated:** 25 May 2018 (to reflect GDPR/DPA 2018); 13 December 2024 (PA/AA regulation by GMC)

#### Key provisions

The guidance sets out **eight principles of confidentiality** and provides a framework for deciding when information can be shared. Key sections include:

- **Paras 1–7:** Ethical and legal duties of confidentiality
- **Paras 9–25:** Disclosing patients' personal information: a framework (including consent, public interest, data protection law)
- **Paras 26–49:** Using and disclosing patient information for direct care
- **Paras 50–76:** Disclosures for the protection of patients and others
- **Paras 77–116:** Using and disclosing patient information for secondary purposes
- **Paras 117–138:** Managing and protecting personal information (including records management and retention, and the rights of patients to access their own records)

Para 1 (exact wording):
> "Trust is an essential part of the relationship between patients and medical professionals and confidentiality is central to this. Patients may avoid seeking medical help, or may under-report symptoms, if they think that their personal information will be disclosed by doctors without consent, or without the chance to have some control over the timing or amount of information shared."

The GMC also publishes six shorter pieces of explanatory guidance on specific situations (disclosures for education/training, employment/insurance, serious communicable diseases, fitness to drive, gunshot/knife wounds, and responding to media criticism).

### What it requires of doctors

Doctors must maintain patient confidentiality as a common law and professional duty. Information can only be disclosed with consent, under a legal basis, or in the public interest. The GMC guidance provides a decision-making framework and specific rules for direct care, secondary uses, and disclosures after death.

## 4. Data Protection Act 2018 / UK GDPR

### 4a. Data Protection Act 2018

- **URL:** https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2018/12/contents
- **Type:** Statute (primary legislation)
- **Commencement:** 25 May 2018

### 4b. UK GDPR (retained EU GDPR as amended for the UK)

- **Guidance (ICO):** https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/a-guide-to-lawful-basis/special-category-data
- **Type:** Statute (retained EU law as amended by the Data Protection, Privacy and Electronic Communications (Amendments etc) (EU Exit) Regulations 2019)

### Key provisions for health records

Health data is classified as **special category data** under Article 9 of the UK GDPR. Special category data requires both:
1. A **lawful basis under Article 6** of the UK GDPR (e.g., (c) legal obligation, (e) public task)
2. A **separate condition under Article 9** for processing special category data

**UK GDPR Article 9(1)** prohibits processing of special category data unless one of the Article 9(2) conditions applies.

**Article 4(15) definition of health data (exact wording):**
> "'data concerning health' means personal data related to the physical or mental health of a natural person, including the provision of health care services, which reveal information about his or her health status"

The ICO confirms health data includes: "any information on injury, disease, disability or disease risk, including medical history, medical opinions, diagnosis and clinical treatment; medical examination data, test results, data from medical devices, or data from fitness trackers; information collected from the individual when they register for health services or access treatment; appointment details, reminders and invoices which tell you something about the health of the individual."

### Article 9(2) conditions for processing special category data (exact list):

| Letter | Condition |
|--------|-----------|
| (a) | Explicit consent |
| (b) | Employment, social security and social protection (if authorised by law) |
| (c) | Vital interests |
| (d) | Not-for-profit bodies |
| (e) | Made public by the data subject |
| (f) | Legal claims or judicial acts |
| (g) | Reasons of substantial public interest (with a basis in law) |
| (h) | Health or social care (with a basis in law) |
| (i) | Public health (with a basis in law) |
| (j) | Archiving, research and statistics (with a basis in law) |

Five of these conditions require additional conditions in UK law, set out in **Schedule 1 of the DPA 2018**:
- Employment, social security and social protection
- Health or social care
- Public health
- Archiving, research and statistics
- Substantial public interest (requires one of 23 specific conditions in Part 2 of Schedule 1)

### Lawful basis for NHS processing of health records

For the provision of direct care, the NHS typically relies on:
- **Article 6(1)(c)** (legal obligation) or **Article 6(1)(e)** (public task — exercise of official authority)
- **Article 9(2)(h)** (health or social care) with the associated condition in **Schedule 1, Part 1, paragraph 2** of the DPA 2018

The ICO states: "In many cases you also need an 'appropriate policy document' in place in order to meet a UK Schedule 1 condition for processing in the DPA 2018."

### What it requires of doctors/organisations

NHS organisations processing health data must identify and document their Article 6 lawful basis and Article 9 condition before processing begins. They must comply with all UK GDPR principles (lawfulness, fairness, transparency; purpose limitation; data minimisation; accuracy; storage limitation; integrity and confidentiality; accountability). Health data requires enhanced security and, in many cases, a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA).

### Recent change: Data (Use and Access) Act 2025

The UK Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 has made some changes to the UK data protection framework, including codifying ICO guidance on subject access requests (requiring a "reasonable and proportionate" search standard) and the "stop the clock" rule for SAR deadlines. The core framework for health data as special category data under Article 9 remains.

## 5. Caldicott Principles

### 5a. Original Caldicott Report (1997)

- **URL (summary):** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1769982/
- **Type:** Report (guidance — produced by the Caldicott Committee chaired by Dame Fiona Caldicott)
- **Published:** 1997

The original report reviewed the transfer of patient-identifiable information within the NHS and produced **six principles** and recommended the appointment of **Caldicott Guardians** in every NHS organisation.

### 5b. Caldicott2 Report (2013)

- **URL (summary):** https://www.hacw.nhs.uk/quality-and-safety/caldicott-principles-943/
- **Type:** Report (guidance — Information Governance Review chaired by Dame Fiona Caldicott)
- **Published:** April 2013

The 2013 review added a **7th principle** ("The duty to share information can be as important as the duty to protect patient confidentiality") and 26 additional recommendations.

### 5c. The Eight Caldicott Principles (current — December 2020)

- **URL:** https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-caldicott-principles
- **PDF:** https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5fcf9b92d3bf7f5d0bb8bb13/Eight_Caldicott_Principles_08.12.20.pdf
- **Type:** Guidance (published by the National Data Guardian)
- **Published:** December 2020 (added an 8th principle)

**Full text of all 8 principles (exact wording from the gov.uk PDF):**

> "Good information sharing is essential for providing safe and effective care. There are also important uses of information for purposes other than individual care, which contribute to the overall delivery of health and social care or serve wider public interests. These principles apply to the use of confidential information within health and social care organisations and when such information is shared with other organisations and between individuals, both for individual care and for other purposes."

**Principle 1: Justify the purpose(s) for using confidential information**
> "Every proposed use or transfer of confidential information should be clearly defined, scrutinised and documented, with continuing uses regularly reviewed by an appropriate guardian."

**Principle 2: Use confidential information only when it is necessary**
> "Confidential information should not be included unless it is necessary for the specified purpose(s) for which the information is used or accessed. The need to identify individuals should be considered at each stage of satisfying the purpose(s) and alternatives used where possible."

**Principle 3: Use the minimum necessary confidential information**
> "Where use of confidential information is considered to be necessary, each item of information must be justified so that only the minimum amount of confidential information is included as necessary for a given function."

**Principle 4: Access to confidential information should be on a strict need-to-know basis**
> "Only those who need access to confidential information should have access to it, and then only to the items that they need to see. This may mean introducing access controls or splitting information flows where one flow is used for several purposes."

**Principle 5: Everyone with access to confidential information should be aware of their responsibilities**
> "Action should be taken to ensure that all those handling confidential information understand their responsibilities and obligations to respect the confidentiality of patient and service users."

**Principle 6: Comply with the law**
> "Every use of confidential information must be lawful. All those handling confidential information are responsible for ensuring that their use of and access to that information complies with legal requirements set out in statute and under the common law."

**Principle 7: The duty to share information for individual care is as important as the duty to protect patient confidentiality**
> "Health and social care professionals should have the confidence to share confidential information in the best interests of patients and service users within the framework set out by these principles. They should be supported by the policies of their employers, regulators and professional bodies."

**Principle 8: Inform patients and service users about how their confidential information is used**
> "A range of steps should be taken to ensure no surprises for patients and service users, so they can have clear expectations about how and why their confidential information is used, and what choices they have about this. These steps will vary depending on the use: as a minimum, this should include providing accessible, relevant and appropriate information - in some cases, greater engagement will be required."

### What it requires of doctors

The Caldicott Principles are guidance (not statute) but are treated as the standard framework for handling confidential patient information in the NHS. Every NHS organisation must have a Caldicott Guardian. Doctors should follow these principles when using or sharing patient information.

## 6. Access to Health Records Act 1990 & Access to Medical Reports Act 1988

### 6a. Access to Health Records Act 1990

- **URL:** https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1990/23/contents
- **Section 3:** https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1990/23/section/3/enacted
- **Type:** Statute (primary legislation)
- **Royal assent:** 13 July 1990

#### Key provisions

**Long title:** "An Act to establish a right of access to health records by the individuals to whom they relate and other persons; to provide for the correction of inaccurate health records..."

**Section 3(1) — Right of access to health records (exact wording):**
> "An application for access to a health record, or to any part of a health record, may be made to the holder of the record by any of the following, namely—
> (a) the patient;
> (b) a person authorised in writing to make the application on the patient's behalf;"

**Scope after amendment:** Most access provisions for living patients were **repealed on 1 March 2000** by the Data Protection Act 1998 (now replaced by the DPA 2018 / UK GDPR). The surviving provisions of the AHRA 1990 now apply primarily to **records of deceased patients**.

Access for deceased patients' records is available to:
- The **personal representative** (executor or administrator) of the deceased
- Any person who **may have a claim arising out of the patient's death**

**Grounds for refusal of access:**
- If access "would be likely to cause serious harm to the physical or mental health of any individual"
- If the records contain information relating to or provided by a third party who could be identified (unless consent given or anonymised)
- If the patient requested before death that access not be given
- Records made before 1 November 1991 (commencement of the Act) are excluded

#### What it requires of doctors

Doctors who are record holders must respond to valid requests from personal representatives or those with a claim arising from death. Access can be limited to information relevant to the claim. A health professional must review records before release.

### 6b. Access to Medical Reports Act 1988

- **URL:** https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/28/contents
- **Type:** Statute (primary legislation)
- **Royal assent:** 29 July 1988
- **In force:** 1 January 1989

#### Key provisions

**Long title:** "An Act to establish a right of access by individuals to reports relating to themselves provided by medical practitioners for employment or insurance purposes and to make provision for related matters."

**Section 1 — Right of access (exact wording):**
> "It shall be the right of an individual to have access, in accordance with the provisions of this Act, to any medical report relating to the individual which is to be, or has been, supplied by a medical practitioner for employment purposes or insurance purposes."

**Section 2 — Definition of "medical report":**
> "'medical report', in the case of an individual, means a report relating to the physical or mental health of the individual prepared by a medical practitioner who is or has been responsible for the clinical care of the individual."

**Section 3 — Consent requirements:**
- The applicant (employer/insurer) must notify the patient of the intention to seek a report
- The patient must consent to the application being made
- The patient must be informed of their rights: to access the report before/after it is supplied, to withhold consent, and to request amendments

**Patients' rights under AMRA 1988:**
1. To withhold permission for the applicant to seek a medical report
2. To access the medical report after completion, either before it is sent or up to **six months** after it is sent
3. If seeing the report before it is sent, to instruct the doctor not to send it
4. To request amendment of inaccuracies in the report

**Grounds for withholding access:**
- If disclosure would be likely to cause serious harm to the physical or mental health of the individual or others
- If the report would reveal the identity of a third party who has not consented
- If the report indicates the practitioner's intentions in respect of the patient

**Section 5 — Amendments:** If the patient believes there are factual inaccuracies, they may apply in writing for amendment. If the doctor disagrees, a note of the patient's views should be appended to the report.

**GMC requirement:** GMC guidance (para 115 of Confidentiality, 2017) states that all doctors, including occupational health physicians, must offer individuals the opportunity to see a report written about them for employment or insurance purposes before it is sent.

#### What it requires of doctors

Doctors must obtain valid consent before providing a medical report, must inform patients of their rights, must allow access to the report (with a 21-day window before sending if the patient wishes to see it), must keep copies for at least six months, and must not omit relevant information at the patient's request.

## 8. Clinical Record-Keeping Standards from Royal Colleges & Academy of Medical Royal Colleges

### 8a. Academy of Medical Royal Colleges (AoMRC) — Standards for the Clinical Structure and Content of Patient Records (2013)

- **URL (AoMRC):** https://www.aomrc.org.uk/
- **Type:** Professional standard (guidance)
- **Published:** 2013 (superseded by PRSB 2018 standards — see below)

The AoMRC published "Standards for the Clinical Structure and Content of Patient Records" in 2013. These were adopted by the Professional Record Standards Body (PRSB) when it was established in 2013.

### 8b. PRSB — Standards for the Structure and Content of Health and Care Records (2018)

- **URL:** https://theprsb.org/standards/
- **PDF:** https://www.rcp.ac.uk/media/3krn3jxl/prsb-structure-and-content-of-health-care-records.pdf
- **Type:** Professional standard (commissioned by NHS Digital, developed with RCP Health Informatics Unit)
- **Published:** July 2018 (replaces the 2013 AoMRC standards)

#### Key provisions

The PRSB standards define the structured content of digital health and care records across multiple use cases:
- **All record structured content** (Section 1): the full set of structured record content used across all use cases
- **Admission record** (Section 2)
- **Handover record** (Section 3)
- **Discharge record** (Section 4)
- **Outpatient letter** (Section 5)
- **Crisis care plan** (Section 6)
- **Clinical referral information** (Section 7)
- **Healthy child record** (Section 8)
- **Digital care and support plan** (Section 9)

The standards specify structured content for: patient demographics, problems and issues, diagnoses, procedures, medications, allergies, relevant clinical findings, clinical risk factors, patient preferences, decisions made, actions agreed, and who is recording the information.

**Key structural elements include:**
- Problems and issues (summary of problems requiring investigation or treatment)
- Diagnoses (including date of onset, severity, certainty)
- Procedures (including anatomical site, laterality, complications)
- Medications (name, dose, route, frequency, duration, status)
- Allergies and adverse reactions
- Clinical summary (brief summary of the encounter)
- Responsible clinician, role, grade, specialty, contact details

The PRSB standards are designed to support interoperability using FHIR (Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources) technology.

### 8c. Other Royal College standards

- **RCP (Royal College of Physicians):** The RCP Health Informatics Unit supports the PRSB standards. The RCP HIU was the original developer of the clinical record structure standards.
- **RCGP (Royal College of General Practitioners):** Provides guidance on record-keeping in general practice, including safeguarding information sharing.
- **RCN (Royal College of Nursing):** Provides standards for nursing documentation and record-keeping.

### What it requires of doctors

The PRSB/AoMRC standards define what structured information should be included in clinical records for different care settings. They are professional standards (not statute) but are endorsed by NHS Digital and expected to be implemented in NHS IT systems.

## 9. Coroners and Justice Act 2009

- **URL:** https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2009/25/contents
- **Section 1:** https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2009/25/section/1
- **Type:** Statute (primary legislation)
- **Commencement:** 25 July 2013 (Part 1 — Coroners)

### Key provisions

**Section 1 — Duty to investigate certain deaths (exact wording):**
> "(1) A senior coroner who is made aware that the body of a deceased person is within that coroner's area must as soon as practicable conduct an investigation into the person's death if subsection (2) applies.
> (2) This subsection applies if the coroner has reason to suspect that—
> (a) the deceased died a violent or unnatural death,
> (b) the cause of death is unknown, or
> (c) the deceased died while in custody or otherwise in state detention."

### Schedule 5, Paragraph 7 — Reports to Prevent Future Deaths (PFD reports)

> "(1) A coroner who has been conducting an investigation... must report a matter to a person who the coroner believes may have the power to take action [if] anything revealed by the investigation gives rise to a concern that circumstances creating a risk of other deaths will occur or continue to exist in the future..."
> "(2) The recipient of the report must respond within 56 days."

### How record-keeping supports it

Clinical records are essential evidence in coroner investigations. Under the **Coroners (Investigations) Regulations 2013**, rule 13, coroners must disclose relevant documents to interested persons, including:
- Post-mortem examination reports
- Any other report provided to the coroner
- Any recording of the inquest
- Any other document the coroner considers relevant

Clinical records are routinely requested by coroners and form the basis of evidence at inquests. Poor record-keeping can lead to adverse findings and PFD reports. The CJA 2009 does not contain a specific "statutory duty of candour" provision — the statutory duty of candour is found in Regulation 20 of the Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014 (see section 11 below). However, the CJA 2009 framework creates an obligation for transparency in death investigations.

### What it requires of doctors

Doctors must provide accurate and complete clinical records when requested by a coroner. Records must be available and legible. Withholding or altering records in relation to a coroner investigation is an offence under Schedule 6 of the CJA 2009.

## 10. CQC Requirements on Records

### 10a. Regulation 17: Good Governance

- **URL:** https://www.cqc.org.uk/guidance-regulation/providers/regulations-service-providers-and-managers/health-social-care-act/regulation-17
- **Type:** Regulation (statutory — part of the Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014)

#### Key provisions — Regulation 17(2)(c) (exact wording from legislation):

> "(2) Without limiting paragraph (1), such systems or processes must enable the registered person, in particular, to—
> ...
> (c) maintain securely an accurate, complete and contemporaneous record in respect of each service user, including a record of the care and treatment provided to the service user and of decisions taken in relation to the care and treatment provided;"

#### CQC guidance on 17(2)(c):

Records must be:
- "Complete, legible, indelible, accurate and up to date, with no undue delays in adding and filing information"
- "Include an accurate record of all decisions taken in relation to care and treatment and make reference to discussions with people who use the service, their carers and those lawfully acting on their behalf. This includes consent records and advance decisions to refuse treatment."
- "Accessible to authorised people as necessary in order to deliver people's care and treatment"
- "Created, amended, stored and destroyed in line with current legislation and nationally recognised guidance"
- "Kept secure at all times and only accessed, amended, or securely destroyed by authorised people"

Both paper and electronic records must comply with the Data Protection Act 2018. Decisions made under the Mental Capacity Act 2005 must be recorded. Systems must support confidentiality and not contravene the DPA 2018.

### 10b. CQC Inspection Framework

CQC assesses services against five key questions:
1. **Are they safe?** (includes whether records are well-maintained)
2. **Are they effective?**
3. **Are they caring?**
4. **Are they responsive?**
5. **Are they well-led?** (includes good governance and records management)

Poor record-keeping is a common cause of CQC regulatory action, including warning notices, requirements for improvement, and in severe cases, prosecution or cancellation of registration.

### What it requires of doctors

Doctors working in CQC-regulated services must maintain accurate, complete, contemporaneous records of care and treatment for each patient. This is a **statutory requirement** under Regulation 17, enforceable by CQC with criminal penalties for breaches.

## 11. Duty of Candour

### 11a. Statutory Duty of Candour — Regulation 20

- **URL (CQC guidance):** https://www.cqc.org.uk/guidance-regulation/providers/regulations-service-providers-and-managers/health-social-care-act/regulation-20
- **URL (legislation):** https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukdsi/2014/9780111117613/regulation/20
- **Type:** Regulation (statutory — part of the Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014)
- **In force:** For NHS trusts since 27 November 2014; for all other CQC-registered providers since 1 April 2015

#### Regulation 20 in full (exact wording from legislation):

> "20.—(1) A health service body must act in an open and transparent way with relevant persons in relation to care and treatment provided to service users in carrying on a regulated activity.
>
> (2) As soon as reasonably practicable after becoming aware that a notifiable safety incident has occurred a health service body must—
> (a) notify the relevant person that the incident has occurred in accordance with paragraph (3), and
> (b) provide reasonable support to the relevant person in relation to the incident, including when giving such notification.
>
> (3) The notification to be given under paragraph (2)(a) must—
> (a) be given in person by one or more representatives of the health service body,
> (b) provide an account of the incident, which to the best of the health service body's knowledge is true of all the facts known at the time of the notification,
> (c) advise the relevant person what further enquiries the health service body believes are appropriate,
> (d) offer an apology, and
> (e) keep a written record of the notification and of all conversations and correspondence with the relevant person in relation to the incident."

**Regulation 20(2)(a) and (3) are criminal offences if breached** — CQC can prosecute directly for failure to notify or failure to keep records of the notification.

#### Definition of "notifiable safety incident" (Regulation 20(8)):
> "any unintended or unexpected incident that occurred in respect of a service user during or as a result of the carrying on of a regulated activity that, in the opinion of a reasonable person, could result in, or appears to have resulted in—
> (a) the death of the service user, or
> (b) severe harm, moderate harm or prolonged psychological harm to the service user"

CQC guidance (updated June 2022, page updated May 2025):
> "The duty of candour is a general duty to be open and transparent with people receiving care from you. It applies to every health and social care provider that CQC regulates."

> "Saying sorry is not an admission of liability."

> "To fulfil the duty of candour, you must apologise for the harm caused, regardless of fault, as well as being open and transparent about what has happened."

### 11b. Professional Duty of Candour (GMC)

- **URL:** https://www.gmc-uk.org/professional-standards/the-professional-standards/good-medical-practice (Domain 2, para 47 — "Being open if things go wrong")
- **URL (GMC/NMC joint guidance):** https://www.gmc-uk.org/ethical-guidance/ethical-guidance-for-doctors/openness-and-honesty-when-things-go-wrong--the-professional-duty-of-candour

The GMC and NMC jointly publish "Openness and honesty when things go wrong: the professional duty of candour." This is professional guidance (not statute) but is binding on GMC registrants. The professional duty applies in all circumstances, not just "notifiable safety incidents."

CQC guidance states:
> "There are two types of duty of candour, statutory and professional... We regulate the statutory duty, while the professional duty is overseen by regulators of specific healthcare professions such as the General Medical Council (GMC), Nursing and Midwifery Council (NMC) and the General Dental Council (GDC)."

> "If something qualifies as a notifiable safety incident, carrying out the professional duty alone will not be enough to meet the requirements of the statutory duty."

### Recent development

In December 2023, the Department of Health and Social Care announced a **review into the effectiveness of the statutory duty of candour** (https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/duty-of-candour-review-terms-of-reference). This review was ongoing as of 2025.

### What it requires of doctors

Doctors must: (a) be open and transparent with patients at all times (professional duty); (b) when a notifiable safety incident occurs, ensure the organisation meets the statutory requirements including verbal notification, written record, apology, and follow-up (statutory duty). Record-keeping is integral — the regulation explicitly requires "a written record of the notification and of all conversations and correspondence."

## 12. Freedom of Information Act 2000 (as it relates to NHS records)

- **URL:** https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2000/36/contents
- **ICO guidance:** https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/foi/what-is-the-foi-act-and-are-we-covered/
- **Type:** Statute (primary legislation)
- **Commencement:** 1 January 2005

### Key provisions

The FOIA 2000 provides a public right of access to information held by public authorities. NHS bodies (including GPs, NHS Trusts, pharmacists, NHS dentists, and opticians) are listed as public authorities in Schedule 1 of the Act.

**ICO guidance:**
> "The Freedom of Information Act 2000 provides public access to information held by public authorities. It does this in two ways:
> * public authorities are obliged to publish certain information about their activities; and
> * members of the public are entitled to request information from public authorities."

**Section 46 Code of Practice** (issued by the Lord Chancellor under FOIA) covers good records management practice. The NHS Records Management Code of Practice 2021 explicitly references this:
> "The FOIA governs access to and management of non-personal public records... Records managers should adhere to the code of practice on record keeping issued by the Secretary of State for Culture, Media and Sport, under section 46 of the FOIA. The section 46 Code of Practice is used as a statutory statement of good practice by the regulator and the courts."

### Key exemption for health records

**Section 40** of the FOIA provides an exemption for personal information — information that constitutes personal data under the DPA 2018 / UK GDPR is exempt from disclosure under FOIA (as it is accessible via subject access requests instead). **Section 41** provides an exemption for information provided in confidence.

### What it requires of doctors/organisations

NHS organisations must maintain records in a way that allows them to respond to FOI requests for non-personal information. Patient-specific information is exempt under FOIA (it is accessed via the UK GDPR subject access request regime instead). NHS bodies must follow the Section 46 Code of Practice on records management.

## Recent Changes (2023–2024)

1. **GMC Good Medical Practice 2024** (in effect 30 January 2024): Record-keeping section expanded — paragraphs 69–71 now include patient preferences/concerns, reasonable adjustments, communication support preferences, and a proportionality principle. New word "contemporaneous" added with definition.

2. **GMC Confidentiality guidance** updated 13 December 2024: Extended to cover physician associates (PAs) and anaesthesia associates (AAs) now regulated by the GMC.

3. **NHS Records Management Code of Practice** updated 5 December 2023: HTML version updated; includes new sections on integrated care, individual rights, personal health records, and social care.

4. **Duty of candour review** announced December 2023: DHSC launched a review into the effectiveness of the statutory duty of candour.

5. **Data (Use and Access) Act 2025**: Updated UK data protection framework, including codified ICO guidance on SAR responses and the "stop the clock" rule.

6. **CQC guidance on Regulation 20** updated June 2022: Clarified how to apply the term "unexpected or unintended" to determine if something qualifies as a notifiable safety incident.
