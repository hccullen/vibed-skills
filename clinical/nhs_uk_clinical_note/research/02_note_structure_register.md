# UK NHS Clinical Note Structure & English Medical Register — Research

> **Purpose:** Primary-source research to inform a skill that helps UK NHS doctors write clinical notes. All claims are traced to official sources (NHS England, NHS Digital, RCP, RCGP, RCEM, NICE, PRSB, GMC, AoMRC). Where a source is a secondary write-up, this is noted.
>
> **Researcher note:** This document distinguishes **official standards** (mandated or endorsed by a national body) from **common practice** (widely used but not formally standardised).

## 1. Clinical Note Dispositions Used in the UK / NHS

### 1.1 SOAP (Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan)

**Origin:** SOAP was developed by Dr Larry Weed in the late 1960s as part of the Problem-Oriented Medical Record (POMR). It is documented in StatPearls (NCBI):

> "Larry Weed developed the widely adopted SOAP note structure almost 50 years ago. The structure reminds clinicians of specific tasks while providing a framework for evaluating information."
>
> — StatPearls, "SOAP Notes," NCBI Bookshelf, NBK482263. <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK482263>

**The four headings** (StatPearls):

> "The 4 headings of a SOAP note are Subjective, Objective, Assessment and Plan."
>
> — ibid.

| Section | Meaning (UK context) |
|---|---|
| **Subjective** | What the patient reports — symptoms, history, concerns. Patient-reported data only. |
| **Objective** | What the clinician observes or measures — vital signs, examination findings, lab results, imaging, screening scores (e.g. NEWS2, AMTS). |
| **Assessment** | The clinician's interpretation — working diagnosis, differential diagnosis, evaluation of progress, risk stratification. The "analytical core." |
| **Plan** | Next steps — treatment changes, medications, referrals, investigations, follow-up timing, patient education, safety-netting advice. |

**UK usage — less universal than North America:**

> "In the UK, SOAP notes are used less universally than in North America. Many UK clinicians use free-text or hybrid formats within their medical record systems, and SOAP is more likely to be encountered in specific settings (such as private practice, dental care, or allied health) than in NHS general practice."
>
> — Tandem Health, "SOAP format in clinical documentation: a practical guide," citing Kiroku's UK-focused documentation guide. <https://tandemhealth.ai/resources/knowledge/soap-format-clinical-documentation-practical-guide> (secondary source)

> "Are SOAP notes used in the NHS? SOAP notes are used less universally in the NHS than in North America. Many NHS clinicians use free-text or hybrid formats within their medical record systems."
>
> — ibid.

**Key distinction for the skill:** In the UK, SOAP is **common practice** in some settings but is **not** an official NHS-mandated note structure. The official structured heading standards come from the PRSB / AoMRC (see §3.3). The critical boundary clinicians must maintain is:

> "The critical distinction is that Objective data is clinician-derived and measurable, whereas Subjective data is patient-reported. Maintaining this boundary matters for clinical clarity."
>
> — ibid.

### 1.2 SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation)

**What it is:** SBAR is a structured communication framework for **handover and urgent communication**, not for progress notes. It is the standard handover communication tool in the NHS.

**NHS Scotland (NHS Education for Scotland) definition:**

> "SBAR is an easy to use, structured communication format that enables information to be transferred accurately between individuals. SBAR stands for 'Situation, background, assessment, recommendation' and was originally developed in the military context to create a reliable consistent process to facilitate concise, clear, focused communication."
>
> — NHS Education for Scotland, "Quality Improvement Zone — SBAR." <https://learn.nes.nhs.scot/3408>

> "SBAR communication is normally very focused and relatively brief. Most SBARs are around one page of A4, two at most."
>
> — ibid.

**The four headings** (NHS Education for Scotland):

> "**1. Situation.** Concisely identify the current situation and give a description of the purpose for this communication. Who are you? Why are you initiating this SBAR? What is happening currently?"
>
> "**2. Background.** Put the current situation into its context. Give a brief overview of the reason you are doing this work/project."
>
> "**3. Assessment.** This is the section where you might go into a little more detail and be very clear about the message you wish to communicate."
>
> "**4. Recommendation.** Your recommendation may vary depending on what stage you are at."
>
> — ibid.

**NHS-recognised clinical example (ectopic pregnancy handover pro forma):**

> "SBAR handover is recognised by the NHS as an easy-to-use, structured form of communication that enables information to be transferred accurately between individuals. It provides a standardised format to ensure staff share concise information which improves communication of key detail and reduces missed information and errors. It is widely used in UK healthcare settings."
>
> — Ectopic Pregnancy Trust, "Handover resource: SBAR," citing NHS England and NHS Improvement, NICE Quality Standard QS174. <https://wp.ectopic.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/SBAR-pdf.pdf>

> "NICE recognises that structured handovers increase positive care outcomes for patients."
>
> — ibid.

**IHI (Institute for Healthcare Improvement) definition** (the original SBAR tool, widely adopted by NHS):

> "**S = Situation** (a concise statement of the problem)
> **B = Background** (pertinent and brief information related to the situation)
> **A = Assessment** (analysis and considerations of options — what you found/think)
> **R = Recommendation** (action requested/recommended — what you want)"
>
> — IHI, "SBAR Tool: Situation-Background-Assessment-Recommendation." <https://www.ihi.org/library/tools/sbar-tool-situation-background-assessment-recommendation>

> "Michael Leonard, MD, Physician Leader for Patient Safety, along with colleagues Doug Bonacum and Suzanne Graham at Kaiser Permanente of Colorado developed this technique."
>
> — ibid.

**WHO SBAR Handover Tool** (used in NHS-aligned contexts):

> "**Situation:** Identify yourself & location; Identify patient (name, age, sex); State diagnosis (suspected or definitive); State reason for transfer or handover.
> **Background:** Admission date; Relevant past medical & surgical history; Recent changes in status; Relevant labs & imaging; Recent vital signs; Management or interventions provided; Relevant psychosocial factors.
> **Assessment:** State the diagnoses or conditions (if diagnostic uncertainty); State severity of illness (stable or critical); State patient trajectory (worsening or improving).
> **Recommendation:** [Action requested]."
>
> — WHO, "SBAR Handover Tool." <https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/integrated-health-services-(ihs)/csy/bec-slides/bec/sbar.pdf>

**NHS England Safe Communication guidance** references SBAR for handover:

> "For prompt cards and other resources to help you use SBAR in several different settings (including acute, primary care and community mental health)…"
>
> — NHS England, "Safe Communication," by N Davey, 2015. <https://www.england.nhs.uk/signuptosafety/wp-content/uploads/sites/16/2015/09/safe-comms-design-implmnt-meas.pdf>

### 1.3 SOAP vs SBAR — When Each Is Used

| Feature | SOAP | SBAR |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary use** | Progress note / encounter documentation | Handover / urgent communication / escalation |
| **Setting** | Ward round entries, outpatient consultations, GP encounters (esp. private/allied health) | Shift handover, referral, escalating a deteriorating patient, inter-service transfer |
| **Length** | Comprehensive (mirrors full clinical reasoning) | Focused and brief (1–2 pages A4 max) |
| **Assessment section** | Diagnostic reasoning, differential, progress evaluation | Analysis of the situation — "what you found/think" |
| **Final section** | **Plan** — treatment, referrals, follow-up, education | **Recommendation** — the specific action requested |
| **Official status in NHS** | Common practice, not officially mandated | Recognised by NHS, NICE QS174, WHO |

> "SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) is widely used for clinical handovers and urgent communications, for example when a nurse escalates a deteriorating patient to a doctor, or when a clinician refers a patient to another service. SBAR is optimised for brevity and clarity in time-sensitive situations, whereas SOAP is better suited to comprehensive encounter documentation."
>
> — Tandem Health (secondary source). <https://tandemhealth.ai/resources/knowledge/soap-format-clinical-documentation-practical-guide>

### 1.4 Admission Clerking Structure

**Definition:** "Clerking" is the UK term for the initial full assessment of a patient on admission, taken by a doctor or advanced nurse practitioner.

> "On arrival at the Acute Medical Unit, patients are seen by a doctor or an advanced nurse practitioner who is responsible for taking a history (including medication), assessing the patient, making a provisional diagnosis, documenting a management plan, ordering initial investigations and writing the admission prescription. This process is known as clerking."
>
> — Jones et al., "Delays and interruptions in the acute medical unit clerking process: an observational study," PMC. <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4737975>

**Standard medical clerking headings (UK common practice):**

| Heading | Content |
|---|---|
| **History of Presenting Complaint (HPC)** | Chronological account of the current symptom(s), onset, character, duration, aggravating/relieving factors, associated symptoms |
| **Past Medical History (PMH)** | Previous illnesses, operations, chronic conditions |
| **Drug History (DH) / Medication History** | Current medications, doses, allergies (document drug + reaction), over-the-counter, adherence |
| **Family History (FH)** | Relevant familial conditions |
| **Social History (SH)** | Smoking, alcohol (units/week), occupation, housing, functional status, illicit drugs, exercise, diet |
| **Systems Review (SR)** | Systematic screen of other body systems |
| **Examination** | Vital signs, system-by-system examination findings, including relevant scores (NEWS2, GCS, AMTS) |
| **Impression** | Working diagnosis / differential diagnosis |
| **Plan** | Investigations, treatment, disposition (admit/discharge), escalation |

> "Patients who are seen initially by a junior doctor are handed over to a senior decision maker (grade ST3 or above) for review immediately following clerking."
>
> — Jones et al., ibid.

The RCP recommends that junior medical staff should be allowed approximately **1 hour** to clerk each new patient (including carrying out interventional procedures, gathering results, and writing a medication chart).

> "The original Royal College of Physicians guidance for establishing Acute Medical Units states that junior medical staff should be allowed 1 h to clerk each new patient including carrying out interventional procedures, gathering results and writing a medication chart."
>
> — ibid., citing RCP guidance.

### 1.5 Standard Scores Documented in UK Notes

#### 1.5.1 NEWS2 (National Early Warning Score)

**Source:** Royal College of Physicians.

> "NEWS2 is the latest version of the National Early Warning Score (NEWS), first produced in 2012 and updated in December 2017, which advocates a system to standardise the assessment and response to acute illness."
>
> — RCP, "National Early Warning Score (NEWS) 2." <https://www.rcp.ac.uk/resources/national-early-warning-score-news-2>

**The six physiological parameters:**

> "The NEWS is based on a simple aggregate scoring system in which a score is allocated to physiological measurements, already recorded in routine practice, when patients present to, or are being monitored in hospital. Six simple physiological parameters form the basis of the scoring system:
> 1. respiration rate
> 2. oxygen saturation
> 3. systolic blood pressure
> 4. pulse rate
> 5. level of consciousness or new confusion*
> 6. temperature.
>
> *The patient has new-onset confusion, disorientation and/or agitation, where previously their mental state was normal — this may be subtle."
>
> — ibid.

**NHS endorsement:**

> "NEWS2 has received formal endorsement from NHS England and NHS Improvement to become the early warning system for identifying acutely ill patients — including those with sepsis — in hospitals in England."
>
> — ibid.

**Clinical response thresholds (RCP, via PMC):**

| NEWS2 Total Score | Frequency of Monitoring | Clinical Response |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Minimum 12-hourly | Continue routine NEWS monitoring |
| 1–4 | Minimum 4–6-hourly | Inform a registered nurse, who must assess the patient; decide whether increased monitoring/escalation required |
| 5–6 | Minimum 1–4-hourly | Urgent review by clinician with competencies; consider moving to environment with monitoring facilities |
| 7 or more | Continuous monitoring | Emergency assessment by critical care team, usually transfer to higher-level care |

> — Adapted from RCP, *National Early Warning Score (NEWS) 2: Standardising the assessment of acute-illness severity in the NHS*. RCP, 2017. Via PMC. <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9761428/>

**Sepsis campaign:**

> "Sepsis should be considered as a potential diagnosis in any acutely ill patient with a NEWS ≥5, and care escalated; with the campaign, 'NEWS2 5 or more — think sepsis'."
>
> — Williams B, "The National Early Warning Score: from concept to NHS implementation," *Clinical Medicine* (2022), PMC. <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9761416>

**Scope:** "NEWS2 should not be used in children under 16 or pregnant women."

> — NICE, "National Early Warning Score systems that alert to deteriorating adult patients in hospital," MIB205. <https://www.nice.org.uk/advice/mib205/chapter/The-technology>

#### 1.5.2 AMTS (Abbreviated Mental Test Score)

**Source:** Hodkinson HM, "Evaluation of a mental test score for assessment of mental impairment in the elderly," *Age and Ageing* 1972;1(4):233–8.

> "The abbreviated mental test score (AMTS) is a 10-point assessment that was introduced by Hodkinson in 1972 to rapidly assess elderly patients for the possibility of dementia. It continues to be used as part of a screening process for both delirium and dementia, although further tests are necessary to confirm these diagnoses."
>
> — Geeky Medics (secondary), citing Hodkinson 1972. <https://geekymedics.com/abbreviated-mental-test-score-amts/>

**The 10 questions** (each correct answer = 1 point):

1. "What is your age?"
2. "What is the time to the nearest hour?"
3. Give the patient an address, and ask them to repeat it at the end of the test (e.g. "42 West Street")
4. "What is the year?"
5. "What is the name of this place?" or "What is your house number?"
6. Can the patient recognise two persons (e.g. doctor, nurse)?
7. "What is your date of birth?" (day and month sufficient)
8. "In what year did World War 1 begin?"
9. "Name the present monarch/prime minister/president"
10. "Count backwards from 20 down to 1"

> — ibid.

**Interpretation:**

> "A score of 6 or less suggests delirium or dementia, although further tests are necessary to confirm the diagnosis."
>
> — ibid.

> "A score <7 suggests that cognitive impairment may be present. This is a screening tool only."
>
> — MDCalc, "Abbreviated Mental Test (AMT-10)." <https://www.mdcalc.com/calc/10208/abbreviated-mental-test-amt-10>

> "The validation of the AMTS followed a large multicenter study of mental impairment in the elderly conducted by the Royal College of Physicians."
>
> — Peters & Howe, "The Abbreviated Mental Test Score; Is There a Need for a Contemporaneous Update?", PMC. <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8634377>

#### 1.5.3 GCS (Glasgow Coma Scale)

**Source:** Teasdale G & Jennett B, "Assessment of coma and impaired consciousness. A practical scale," *Lancet* 1974;2:81–84. Developed at the University of Glasgow, Scotland.

> "The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) was first created by Graham Teasdale and Bryan Jennett in 1974. It is a clinical scale to assess a patient's 'depth and duration of impaired consciousness and coma' following an acute brain injury."
>
> — Physiopedia (secondary), citing Teasdale & Jennett 1974. <https://www.physio-pedia.com/Glasgow_Coma_Scale>

**Three components (StatPearls):**

> "The GCS evaluates the following 3 parameters of responsiveness: best eye response (E), best verbal response (V), and best motor response (M). The levels of response in the components of the GCS are scored from 1 (no response) up to normal values of 4 (eye-opening response), 5 (verbal response), and 6 (motor response), respectively. The total GCS score ranges from 3 to 15, with 3 being the lowest and 15 being the highest."
>
> — StatPearls, "Glasgow Coma Scale," NCBI. <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK513298>

**Scoring:**

| Component | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Eyes** | Does not open | To pain | To voice | Spontaneously | — | — |
| **Verbal** | No sounds | Incomprehensible sounds | Inappropriate words | Confused | Oriented | — |
| **Motor** | No movement | Extension to pain | Abnormal flexion to pain | Withdrawal to pain | Localises pain | Obeys commands |

> — CDC, citing Teasdale & Jennett 1974. <https://www.cdc.gov/dengue/training/cme/ccm/page57206.html>

> "Clinicians should denote any untestable component of the GCS as 'NT' (not testable) and avoid using the total score when a component is not testable."
>
> — StatPearls, ibid.

**GCS is documented as component scores (e.g. E4V5M6 = 15) and is a standard part of UK neurological observations.**

#### 1.5.4 SIRS (Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome)

SIRS criteria are widely documented in UK clinical notes as part of sepsis assessment, though the current NHS sepsis guidance now focuses on NEWS2 ≥5 as the primary trigger ("NEWS2 5 or more — think sepsis"). SIRS itself is a clinical concept (two or more of: temperature >38°C or <36°C, heart rate >90, respiratory rate >20 or PaCO2 <32, WCC >12 or <4). This is common practice rather than a standalone official NHS standard.

### 1.6 Discharge Summary Structure

**Official standard:** The PRSB eDischarge Summary Standard (now owned and managed by NHS England from 1 January 2026).

> "The eDischarge Summary Standard enables hospitals to safely transfer accurate clinical information and coded data to GP surgeries, care homes and community services when a patient is discharged. It ensures all relevant information about the patient, including diagnoses, medications, procedures and allergies, is shared in order to improve the quality and consistency of care."
>
> — PRSB, "eDischarge Summary Standard." <https://theprsb.org/standards/edischargesummary/>

> "This standard defines the information content and structure that should be used to create a hospital discharge. It is designed for sending coded and structured electronic discharges which can be transferred to primary care and other systems and used to populate their record systems."
>
> — ibid.

> "From 01 January 2026, this standard will be owned and managed by NHS England and is made available for reuse or amendment under the Open Government Licence v3.0 (OGL 3.0)."
>
> — ibid.

**Mandatory, required, and optional fields:**

> "A mandatory field in the eDischarge summary must be completed. A required field should be recorded if you have the information available and is important to complete for the safety and care of the patient. It is your decision to complete information that is optional."
>
> — PRSB, "eDischarge summaries — a guide." <https://theprsb.org/edischarge-summaries-a-guide/>

> "Patient demographic information is an example of mandatory information that must be completed such as a patient's name, date of birth and NHS number."
>
> — ibid.

**RCPCH endorsement (paediatric):**

> "Standard 7: When a child presents with unscheduled care needs the discharge summary is sent electronically to their GP and other relevant healthcare professionals within 24 hours and the information is given to the child and their parents and carers."
>
> — RCPCH, "Electronic discharge summary within 24 hours — PRSB eDischarge summary." <https://www.rcpch.ac.uk/resources/electronic-discharge-summary-within-24-hours-prsb-edischarge-summary>

**TTO (To Take Out):** TTO refers to the discharge prescription (medications "to take out") that accompanies the patient on discharge. The discharge letter/summary is the clinical communication to the GP. Both are standard NHS practice.

## 2. English Medical Register and Terminology Conventions

### 2.1 UK vs US Medical Spelling

British English (BrE) medical spelling differs from American English (AmE) in several systematic ways. The UK NHS uses British English spellings throughout clinical documentation. Key patterns (source: translation-clinic.com, a medical translation reference, and Alamma.eu medical terminology guide):

**`ae` vs `e`** (BrE retains the `ae` diphthong):

| British English (UK/NHS) | American English |
|---|---|
| anaemia | anemia |
| anaesthetic | anesthetic |
| aetiology | etiology |
| caesarean | cesarean |
| defaecation | defecation |
| dyslipidaemia | dyslipidemia |
| gynaecology | gynecology |
| haemoglobin | hemoglobin |
| haemorrhage | hemorrhage |
| ischaemic | ischemic |
| leukaemia | leukemia |
| orthopaedic | orthopedic |
| paediatric | pediatric |

> "Many medical terms spelled with 'ae' in British English are spelled with 'e' in American English."
>
> — translation-clinic.com, "Spelling of medical terms in British and American English." <https://translation-clinic.com/spelling-medical-terms-british-american-english>

**`oe` vs `e`** (BrE retains the `oe` diphthong):

| British English (UK/NHS) | American English |
|---|---|
| diarrhoea | diarrhea |
| coeliac | celiac |
| dyspnoea | dyspnea |
| oedema | edema |
| oesophagus | esophagus |
| oestrogen | estrogen |
| gonorrhoea | gonorrhea |

> — ibid.

> "Fetus: 'Fetus' is the preferred spelling in the BMJ and most other publications. Oxford Dictionaries lists foetus as a 'variant spelling of fetus (chiefly in British non-technical use)'."
>
> — ibid.

**`our` vs `or`** (BrE retains the `u`):

| British English | American English |
|---|---|
| behaviour | behavior |
| colour | color |
| labour | labor |
| tumour | tumor |

> — ibid.

**`re` vs `er`** (BrE retains `re`):

| British English | American English |
|---|---|
| centre | center |
| fibre | fiber |
| litre | liter |
| theatre | theater |

> — ibid.

**`ise` vs `ize`** — In BrE, both `-ise` and `-ize` are accepted; in AmE only `-ize`. However, some words can only end in `-ise` in both: exercise, advise, revise, promise. UK medical writing conventionally uses `-ise` (e.g. "immunise," "categorise").

> — ibid.

**Other notable differences:**

| British English | American English |
|---|---|
| aluminium | aluminum |
| mould | mold |
| per cent | percent |
| practise (verb), practice (noun) | practice (both) |
| licence (noun), license (verb) | license (both) |

> — ibid.

### 2.2 Latin/Greek vs Plain English Terms in UK Practice

UK clinical writing retains a substantial Latin/Greek medical vocabulary alongside plain English. The NHS Service Manual (for patient-facing content) explicitly prefers plain English, but clinical notes use both registers:

**NHS Service Manual (patient-facing content) preferences:**

> "We use 'kidney' instead of 'renal'."
> "We prefer 'sudden', 'starts suddenly' … We do use 'acute' in the names of conditions like 'acute lymphoblastic leukaemia'."
> "We prefer the words 'high temperature' to 'fever' … Our user research shows that 'fever' is not a well understood word."
> "We prefer 'sleepy' to 'drowsy'."
> "We prefer 'farting' to 'flatulence'. We only use 'flatulence' when we need to for clinical content."

> — NHS Service Manual, "A to Z of NHS health writing." <https://service-manual.nhs.uk/content/a-to-z-of-nhs-health-writing>

**However, in clinical notes (doctor-to-doctor communication), Latin/Greek terms remain standard:**
- "status epilepticus," "erythema," "dyspnoea," "pyrexia," "erythema multiforme," "haematemesis," "melaena"
- The GMC and royal colleges do not prohibit Latin/Greek terminology in clinical notes — they are the shared professional vocabulary.

> — NHS, "Understanding abbreviations in the NHS App." <https://www.nhs.uk/nhs-app/help/understanding-abbreviations/>

>

**Dangerous abbreviations — ISMP and Joint Commission guidance (adopted in UK practice):**

The Joint Commission "Do Not Use" list (widely adopted internationally including UK trusts) prohibits certain abbreviations that are easily confused:

- **U or u** (unit) → write "unit" (can be confused with 0)
- **IU** (international unit) → write "international unit" (can be confused with IV)
- **Q.D, QD, q.d, qd** (daily) → write "daily" (can be confused with QID)
- **Q.O.D, QOD, q.o.d, qod** (every other day) → write "every other day" (can be confused with QD)
- **Trailing zero (X.0 mg)** → write "X mg" (decimal point can be missed)
- **Lack of leading zero (.X mg)** → write "0.X mg" (decimal point can be missed)
- **MS** → write "morphine sulfate" or "magnesium sulfate" (ambiguous)
- **MgSO4** vs **MSO4** → ambiguous

> — Joint Commission, "Do Not Use List/Prohibited Abbreviations." <https://www.jointcommission.org/en-us/knowledge-library/support-center/standards-interpretation/do-not-use-list-of-abbreviations>

> "The Institute for Safe Medication Practices has identified and published additional error-prone abbreviations."
>
> — ibid.

**NHS practice note:** Individual NHS trusts maintain local "do not use" abbreviation lists based on the ISMP/Joint Commission guidance. There is no single NHS-wide abbreviation standard for clinical notes, but the ISMP principles are widely adopted.

### 2.4 Register and Tone

**GMC Good Medical Practice (2024) — para 67-70, "Recording your work clearly, accurately, and legibly":**

> "67 You must record your work clearly, accurately, and legibly in the patient's record or other relevant documents, as soon as possible after the event to be recorded."
>
> — GMC, *Good Medical Practice* (2024). <https://www.gmc-uk.org/professional-standards/the-professional-standards/good-medical-practice>

> "68 Clinical records should include:
> a relevant clinical findings
> b the decisions made and actions agreed, and who is making the decisions and agreeing the actions
> c the information shared with patients
> d concerns or preferences expressed by the patient that might be relevant to their ongoing care, and whether these were addressed
> e information about any reasonable adjustments and communication support preferences
> f decisions made, actions agreed (including decisions to take no action) and when/whether decisions should be reviewed
> g who is creating the record and when."
>
> — ibid. (from the 2024 GMP PDF, <https://www.gmc-uk.org/cdn/documents/good-medical-practice-2024---english_pdf-102607294.pdf>)

> "Documents you make (including clinical records) to formally record your work must be clear, accurate and legible. You should make records at the same time as the events you are recording or as soon as possible afterwards."
>
> — GMC, *Good Medical Practice* (earlier edition, via UK Parliament). <https://qna.files.parliament.uk/qna-attachments/1676141/original/Good-medical-practice.pdf>

**Tone conventions (UK common practice):**
- Third person, objective: "The patient presented with…" not "My patient presented with…"
- Factual and non-judgmental
- Contemporaneous (made at the time of or soon after the event)
- Attributed (who made the record and when)

> "Medical records are made to support safe and effective care but they may be used for other purposes. […] They may also be used as evidence in court."
>
> — GMC, cited by Medical Protection. <https://www.medicalprotection.org/uk/guidance/medical-records>

> "Good medical records should be legible and clear and contain the following information: Relevant medical history; Examination and other relevant clinical findings — include important positives and negatives and details of objective measurements such as blood pressure; Differential diagnosis; Investigations — details of any investigations requested."
>
> — Medical Protection, "Guide to record keeping in medical practice and healthcare." <https://www.medicalprotection.org/uk/guidance/medical-records>

## 3. NHS Clinical Documentation Standards

### 3.1 PRSB (Professional Record Standards Body)

**What it is:**

> "The Professional Record Standards Body (PRSB) is a body responsible for the development of standards enabling interoperability for clinical record sharing across health and care. It is a member organisation bringing together 85 members, including royal colleges and social care organisations."
>
> — UK Health Data Research Alliance. <https://ukhealthdata.org/members/prsb/>

> "PRSB record standards exist to support the safe and efficient exchange of information across health and care services."
>
> — PRSB, "PRSB Standards." <https://theprsb.org/standards/>

**The PRSB Standards for the Structure and Content of Health and Care Records (July 2018):**

This is the foundational document for UK clinical record structure. It was commissioned by NHS Digital and developed with the RCP Health Informatics Unit. It adopted and extended the 2013 AoMRC "Standards for the Clinical Structure and Content of Patient Records."

## 4. Observation vs Interpretation, Hedging / Uncertainty Vocabulary

### 4.1 Distinguishing Observation from Interpretation

UK clinical notes distinguish:
- **Observation (objective data):** what was measured, examined, or tested — vital signs, examination findings, lab results, imaging
- **Interpretation (assessment):** the clinician's synthesis — working diagnosis, differential, clinical impression

The PRSB standard enforces this by separating "Problems and issues" (observations/findings) from "Diagnoses" (interpretation), and the NHS England GP guidance separates "signs" (objective) from "symptoms" (subjective) from "suspected diagnosis" (interpretation).

### 4.2 Hedging and Uncertainty Vocabulary

UK clinical practice uses specific vocabulary to mark the degree of diagnostic certainty:

| Term | Meaning | Level of certainty |
|---|---|---|
| **"query"** or **"?"** | Used as a prefix to flag a possible but unconfirmed diagnosis (e.g. "?PE", "query meningitis") | Very low — investigation needed |
| **"possible"** | A differential that cannot be excluded | Low–moderate |
| **"probable"** | More likely than not but not confirmed | Moderate–high |
| **"likely"** | The most probable explanation | Moderate–high |
| **"suspected"** | Clinical suspicion sufficient to trigger investigation or treatment (e.g. "suspected sepsis", "suspected cancer") | Moderate — action warranted |
| **"presumed"** | Assumed in the absence of evidence to the contrary | Moderate |
| **"working diagnosis"** | The current leading differential, subject to revision with new evidence | Moderate — explicitly revisable |
| **"differential diagnosis"** / **"DDx"** | List of plausible alternative diagnoses | Varying |
| **"impression"** | The clinician's synthesis of the presentation | Varying |

**"Working diagnosis" — RCP Edinburgh:**

> "We suggest that the routine use of the term 'working diagnosis' would draw attention to [metacognition and Bayesian] strategies by emphasising the uncertainty that prevails. Even when all relevant tests have been made, some uncertainty may remain. The task of the clinician is, therefore, not necessarily to attain certainty, but rather to reduce the level of diagnostic uncertainty enough to make optimal therapeutic decisions."
>
> "The 'working diagnosis' is simply the differential with the greater probability. By using the term 'working diagnosis', the clinician is challenged to re-evaluate the diagnosis in light of new evidence. In this way, anchoring bias, confirmation bias and premature closure bias may be avoided. Clinicians should embrace uncertainty whilst avoiding decision paralysis in the face of uncertainty."
>
> — Sullivan SJ & Whyte MB, "'Working diagnosis' as a means to reduce cognitive bias," *Journal of the Royal College of Physicians of Edinburgh* 2019;49(2):171–174. <https://openresearch.surrey.ac.uk/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Working-diagnosis-as-a-means-to/99510935102346>

**NICE definition of safety-netting (which explicitly involves uncertainty):**

> "The provision of support for patients in whom the clinician has some uncertainty as to whether the patient has a self-limiting illness and is concerned that their condition may deteriorate."
>
> — NICE, cited in BMJ Quality & Safety. <https://qualitysafety.bmj.com/content/31/7/541>

### 4.3 UK Documentation Conventions for Uncertainty

- **Prefix "?"** is the most common shorthand: "?PE", "?appendicitis", "?sepsis" — placed before a suspected diagnosis
- **"Query"** is the written equivalent: "query cellulitis"
- These mark the entry as a **suspected** or **provisional** diagnosis, not an established fact
- The distinction matters for coding: the PRSB standard separates "Problems and issues" (which may include unconfirmed findings) from "Diagnoses" (which should be confirmed)

## 5. Safety-Netting and Follow-Up Language

### 5.1 Definition and Origin

**Origin:** The term "safety netting" in the primary care context was coined by Roger Neighbour in 1987:

> "Roger Neighbour who described it as a back-up process for dealing with uncertainty in primary care whereby the general practitioner (GP) asks themselves three questions when making a preliminary diagnosis: 'If I'm right, what do I expect to happen? How will I know if I am wrong? What would I do then?'"
>
> — BMJ Quality & Safety, "Optimising GPs' communication of advice to facilitate patients' self-care and prompt follow-up when the diagnosis is uncertain." <https://qualitysafety.bmj.com/content/31/7/541>

**NICE definition:**

> "The provision of support for patients in whom the clinician has some uncertainty as to whether the patient has a self-limiting illness and is concerned that their condition may deteriorate."
>
> — NICE, cited ibid.

### 5.2 Documentation Requirements

**Safety-netting must be documented in the record:**

> "When safety-netting advice is documented in sufficient detail in the patient's record, clinicians caring for the patient in the future are aware of what has been discussed and decided, so can continue care effectively. Thorough recording of safety-netting advice was also highlighted as important from a medico-legal perspective."
>
> — ibid.

**Key safety-netting documentation elements (from the BMJ Quality & Safety realist review):**

1. **Specific symptoms to watch for** (not vague "come back if worse") — "red flags"
2. **Timeframe** for expected resolution or when to return
3. **How to seek further help** (who to contact, which service)
4. **Explicit invitation to return** even for the same symptoms (legitimises re-consultation)
5. **What to do if symptoms persist or recur** — even if previous tests were negative
6. **Record that the patient understood the advice**

> "The recommendation to describe specific warning symptoms or signs of serious disease was regarded as important and necessary. Careful, rather than avoidant, communication, of these 'red flags', was thought best to avoid unnecessary distress or anxiety."
>
> — Almond et al., "Quality improvements of safety-netting guidelines for cancer in UK primary care," PMC. <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6833915>

> "When advising about re-consultation, GPs said it was the patient's responsibility to make follow-up appointments."
>
> — ibid.

**Common pitfalls in safety-netting documentation:**

> "The second most common symptom patients were told to look out for was if their symptoms persisted, but only one in five cases recorded that they had provided a timeframe."
>
> — PMC, "Safety netting in primary care." <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11789814/>

> "Generic safety netting for children is often 'too vague to be useful'."
>
> — ibid.

### 5.3 Follow-Up Language Conventions

- **"Safety-net advice given"** — must be accompanied by specifics (symptoms, timeframe, action)
- **"To be reviewed in [clinic/ward] in [X days/weeks]"**
- **"Re-refer if [specific symptoms] persist or recur"**
- **"Three strikes and you are in"** — informal but used in cancer safety-netting guidance (if recurrent unexplained symptoms, refer even if previous tests negative)
- **"If symptoms do not resolve, or persist intermittently, further investigations should be conducted even if previous tests were negative and referral considered"**

> "If symptoms do not resolve, or persist intermittently, further investigations should be conducted even if previous tests were negative and referral considered, such as 'three strikes and you are in'."
>
> — Almond et al., ibid.

## 6. Closed-Loop Communication / Critical Results

### 6.1 Closed-Loop Communication

**Definition (AHRQ TeamSTEPPS):**

> "Closed-loop communication uses verbal feedback to ensure that messages are correctly understood by recipients using methods including call-outs, check-backs, and teach-back. Closed loop communication is a three-step strategy: sender initiates message, receiver accepts message and provides feedback confirmation, and sender verifies message was received."
>
> — AHRQ, "Tool: Closed-Loop Communication." <https://www.ahrq.gov/teamstepps-program/curriculum/communication/tools/loop.html>

**Three steps:**

1. **Call-out:** Sender initiates a clear message, directed to a specific person
2. **Check-back (read-back):** Receiver repeats the message back verbatim
3. **Confirm:** Sender verifies the repeat-back was correct ("That's correct")

> "In closed-loop communication, the person receiving instruction or information repeats it back to make sure the message is understood correctly, and the sender confirms to 'close the loop.'"
>
> — AHA TeamSTEPPS Video Toolkit. <https://www.aha.org/center/project-firstline/teamstepps-video-toolkit/closed-loop-communication>

**UK/NHS context:** Closed-loop communication is used in UK clinical settings (resuscitation, drug administration, handover) and is taught as part of human factors and non-technical skills training. It is **common practice** in UK clinical training, aligned with the TeamSTEPPS framework that NHS organisations adopt.

### 6.2 Documenting Critical Results / Handover

**NHS England Safe Communication guidance** (2015) covers handover and result communication:

> "Making transfers of care simple will reduce the risk (improve quality) and release time to care (improve productivity)."
>
> — NHS England, "Safe Communication," by N Davey, 2015. <https://www.england.nhs.uk/signuptosafety/wp-content/uploads/sites/16/2015/09/safe-comms-design-implmnt-meas.pdf>

**Critical results documentation conventions (UK common practice):**

When documenting handover of critical results, the note should capture:
1. **Who** was informed (name and grade)
2. **When** they were informed (date and time)
3. **What** was communicated (the result and its significance)
4. **The response** — what action was agreed
5. **Read-back / confirmation** that the information was received correctly

**Critical test results — Joint Commission / AHRQ guidance (adopted in UK):**

> "Incomplete or delayed test result communication is a known factor in diagnostic error."
>
> — AHRQ PSNet, "Advancing safety with closed-loop communication of test results." <https://psnet.ahrq.gov/issue/advancing-safety-closed-loop-communication-test-results>

**No single Danish VAF/VUF/VIF equivalent exists as a named standard in the NHS.** The equivalent is the combination of:
- SBAR for the communication
- Closed-loop communication (read-back/confirm) for verification
- Documenting who was informed, when, and what action was agreed (per GMC record-keeping requirements)

## Summary: Official Standards vs Common Practice

| Area | Official Standard | Common Practice |
|---|---|---|
| **Note structure** | PRSB Standards for Structure & Content of Health and Care Records (2018), owned by NHS England from 2026 | SOAP used in some settings; free-text/hybrid in much of NHS GP practice |
| **Handover** | SBAR (NHS-recognised, NICE QS174) | SBAR widely used |
| **Admission clerking** | PRSB Admission Record headings | Traditional HPC/PMH/DH/FH/SH/SR/Exam/Impression/Plan |
| **Discharge** | PRSB eDischarge Summary Standard (v2.1, NHS England from 2026) | TTO + discharge letter |
| **Early warning** | NEWS2 (RCP 2017, NHS England endorsed) | Universal in acute trusts |
| **Cognitive screen** | AMTS (Hodkinson 1972, RCP-validated) | 4AT increasingly used for delirium |
| **Consciousness** | GCS (Teasdale & Jennett 1974, Glasgow) | Universal |
| **Record-keeping** | GMC Good Medical Practice (2024), paras 67–70 | — |
| **GP records** | NHS England Good Practice Guidelines for GP Electronic Patient Records; SNOMED CT mandated | CARAT principles |
| **Terminology** | SNOMED CT (mandated in primary care) | British English spelling throughout |
| **Abbreviations** | ISMP/Joint Commission "Do Not Use" list (adopted by trusts); NHS App abbreviation glossary | Local trust "do not use" lists |
| **Uncertainty** | No single standard | "?", "query", "possible", "probable", "likely", "suspected", "working diagnosis" |
| **Safety-netting** | NICE definition; RCGP curriculum | Documentation of specific symptoms, timeframes, actions |
| **Closed-loop comms** | AHRQ TeamSTEPPS (adopted by NHS orgs) | Call-out → check-back → confirm |
