# NHS UK Clinical Note — Language, Style, and Structure

Write NHS UK clinical notes that read as if a UK-trained doctor wrote them for a colleague who will take over the patient's care. The record is a **communication tool between professionals** — and the language must serve that function above all else.

The governing standard is **Good Medical Practice (GMC, 2024)**, paragraph 69: formal records must be "clear, accurate, contemporaneous and legible." Records that fall below this standard — vague, ambiguous, or uninterpretable to a colleague — are criticisable even when the clinical decision was sound, and may put a doctor's registration at risk (GMC fitness to practise) or breach the **statutory** record-keeping duty under Regulation 17 of the Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014 (enforceable by the CQC).

This file is sufficient for most clinical notes. Sub-pages at the end list the specific situations that require loading them.

## Note dispositions — SOAP, SBAR, and clerking

UK practice uses several dispositions depending on the context. Choose the one that fits the purpose:

### SOAP — for encounter and progress notes

**SOAP** (Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan) structures a progress or encounter note so a colleague can scan it and act. It is **common practice** in some UK settings (private practice, allied health, dentistry) but is **not an official NHS-mandated structure** — the official structured headings come from the PRSB (see below). Where SOAP is used:

- **S — Subjective:** What the patient reports — symptoms, history, concerns. Patient-reported data only.
- **O — Objective:** What the clinician observes or measures — vital signs, examination findings, lab results, imaging, screening scores (NEWS2, AMTS, GCS).
- **A — Assessment:** The clinician's interpretation — working diagnosis, differential diagnosis, evaluation of progress, risk stratification. The analytical core.
- **P — Plan:** Next steps — treatment changes, medications, referrals, investigations, follow-up timing, patient education, safety-netting advice.

The critical boundary: **Objective** is clinician-derived and measurable; **Subjective** is patient-reported. Maintain this distinction.

### SBAR — for handover and urgent communication

**SBAR** (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) is the **NHS-recognised** handover and escalation tool (endorsed by NHS England, NICE QS174, WHO). Use it for shift handover, referral, escalating a deteriorating patient, and inter-service transfer — **not** for comprehensive encounter documentation. Keep it brief: one to two pages of A4 at most.

- **S — Situation:** A concise statement of the problem. Who you are, why you are calling, what is happening now. Identify the patient (name, age, NHS number), state the suspected or definitive diagnosis, and the reason for the handover.
- **B — Background:** Pertinent and brief context — admission date, relevant past medical and surgical history, recent changes in status, relevant labs and imaging, recent vital signs, management provided.
- **A — Assessment:** Your analysis of the situation — severity (stable or critical), trajectory (worsening or improving), diagnostic uncertainty.
- **R — Recommendation:** The specific action requested — what you want the recipient to do, and by when.

| Feature | SOAP | SBAR |
|---|---|---|
| Primary use | Progress note / encounter | Handover / escalation |
| Length | Comprehensive | Focused, brief (1–2 pages) |
| Final section | Plan (treatment, follow-up) | Recommendation (specific action) |
| NHS status | Common practice | Recognised (NICE QS174) |

### Admission clerking

"Clerking" is the UK term for the initial full assessment on admission. The standard headings (common practice, aligned with the PRSB admission record):

| Heading | Content |
|---|---|
| **History of Presenting Complaint (HPC)** | Chronological account of current symptoms — onset, character, duration, aggravating/relieving factors, associated symptoms |
| **Past Medical History (PMH)** | Previous illnesses, operations, chronic conditions |
| **Drug History (DH)** | Current medications, doses, **allergies (document drug + reaction)**, over-the-counter, adherence |
| **Family History (FH)** | Relevant familial conditions |
| **Social History (SH)** | Smoking, alcohol (units/week), occupation, housing, functional status, illicit drugs, exercise, diet |
| **Systems Review (SR)** | Systematic screen of other body systems |
| **Examination** | Vital signs, system-by-system findings, relevant scores (NEWS2, GCS, AMTS) |
| **Impression** | Working diagnosis / differential diagnosis |
| **Plan** | Investigations, treatment, disposition (admit/discharge), escalation |

### PRSB structured headings — the official standard

The **Professional Record Standards Body (PRSB)** "Standards for the Structure and Content of Health and Care Records" (2018, commissioned by NHS Digital, developed with the RCP Health Informatics Unit) define the official structured record headings across use cases: admission, handover, discharge, outpatient letter, referral, crisis care plan, and healthy child record. Where the record system supports structured entry, use the PRSB headings. Key headings include: problems and issues, diagnoses, procedures, medications, allergies and adverse reactions, relevant clinical risk factors, decisions made and actions agreed, and the person completing the record (role, grade, specialty, contact details).

The PRSB separates **"Problems and issues"** (symptoms, signs, examination findings — not diagnoses) from **"Diagnoses"** (confirmed and working diagnoses). Respect this distinction: an unconfirmed finding is a problem, not a diagnosis.

## Register — the voice of the record

Write in a **factual, professional register** that a colleague can scan and act on. The record is not a narrative; it is a working tool. Every sentence should convey information a colleague needs — not what the writer thought, felt, or intended, but what was observed, decided, and done.

**British English is the language of the record.** Write the note in British English. Use the spellings a UK-trained colleague expects:

| British English (UK/NHS) | Do not use (American) |
|---|---|
| oedema, paediatrics, gynaecology, orthopaedic, anaemia, haemoglobin, haemorrhage, ischaemic, leukaemia, aetiology, anaesthetic, caesarean, dyslipidaemia | edema, pediatrics, gynecology, orthopedic, anemia, hemoglobin, hemorrhage, ischemic, leukemia, etiology, anesthetic, cesarean, dyslipidemia |
| diarrhoea, oesophagus, oestrogen, dyspnoea, oedema, coeliac, gonorrhoea | diarrhea, esophagus, estrogen, dyspnea, edema, celiac, gonorrhea |
| tumour, colour, labour, behaviour | tumor, color, labor, behavior |
| centre, fibre, litre, theatre | center, fiber, liter, theater |
| per cent | percent |
| mould | mold |
| immunise, categorise | immunize, categorize |

Use Latin and Greek anatomical and diagnostic terms where they are the established professional vocabulary — *status epilepticus*, *erythema*, *dyspnoea*, *pyrexia*, *haematemesis*, *melaena* — because these are the terms a UK-trained colleague expects. In **patient-facing** content, the NHS Service Manual prefers plain English (*kidney* not *renal*, *high temperature* not *fever*); in **clinical notes** (doctor-to-doctor), the professional terms remain standard.

The record must be **unambiguous and structured** so information can be found quickly.

## Abbreviations — the comprehension test

There is **no single NHS-wide abbreviation standard** for clinical notes, but the ISMP / Joint Commission "Do Not Use" list is widely adopted by NHS trusts, and the BNF sets safe-prescribing rules. The test is whether the abbreviation is understandable to other professionals involved in the patient's care.

**Acceptable in clinical notes:**
- Internationally standardised abbreviations widely used in UK practice — *BP*, *ECG*, *MRI*, *CT*, *CRP*, *INR*, *FBC*, *U&E*, *NEWS2*, *GCS*, *AMTS*
- UK clinical shorthand any UK healthcare professional would recognise — *A&E*, *ED*, *PMH*, *DH*, *FH*, *SH*, *HPC*, *DNACPR*, *TTO*, *TTA*, *OPD*, *WARD*
- Traditional pharmaceutical abbreviations on prescriptions — see [PRESCRIBING.md](PRESCRIBING.md)

**Do not use (NPSA / NHS England enduring standards and BNF):**

| Do not use | Use instead | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| U or IU | units | "U" mistaken for "0"; "IU" confused with "IV" |
| μg or mcg | micrograms | misread as "mg" (1000× error) |
| ng (abbreviated) | nanograms | write in full |
| OD (for once daily) | once daily | "OD" can mean "right eye" (oculus dexter) |
| c.c. or cm³ | mL | should not be used in medicine |
| 3.0 mg | 3 mg | trailing zero misread as 30 |
| .5 mL | 0.5 mL | always include a leading zero |
| MS | morphine sulfate / magnesium sulfate | ambiguous |
| QD, QOD | daily / every other day | confused with QDS |

**Not acceptable:** specialty-internal abbreviations, personal shorthand, or non-standard letter-and-slash combinations that a colleague from another specialty cannot decode.

## Templates and macros — check against what actually happened

Templates and macros may be used in the record where appropriate. Every template insertion must be checked against what actually happened. If a template says *"Patient has been given verbal and written information about the procedure, including risks of complications, and has given informed consent"* — this must be literally true for this patient at this contact. If the information given differed from what the template describes, edit the template to reflect what was actually said. Do not let a macro assert facts that are not true for this patient.

## Objectivity — observation vs interpretation

Separate what was **observed** from what was **inferred**. The record captures both, but the language must make the distinction visible.

**Observed** — state as fact, in the present or past tense:
- *"Palpable mass in right upper quadrant"*
- *"Patient is alert and oriented"*
- *"Blood pressure 145/90, pulse 78, temp 37.2"*

**Inferred** — mark as clinical assessment, not as established fact:
- *"Suspected cholecystitis"* — not *"cholecystitis"* unless confirmed
- *"Likely functional symptoms"* — not *"functional symptoms"*
- *"Assessed as having reduced compliance"* — not *"reduced compliance"*

In the **O** section of SOAP, state what you found, not the process of examining. Not *"I palpated the abdomen and found..."* — write *"Abdomen soft, no palpable masses, no rebound tenderness."*

The PRSB enforces this by separating "Problems and issues" (observations/findings) from "Diagnoses" (interpretation), and NHS England GP guidance separates "signs" (objective) from "symptoms" (subjective) from "suspected diagnosis" (interpretation).

## Hedging and uncertainty — calibrate the epistemic level

UK clinical writing uses a calibrated vocabulary for certainty. Match the word to the evidence:

| Term | Meaning | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| *confirmed* | established | confirmed by investigation |
| *query* or *?* | suspected, unconfirmed | clinical suspicion, investigation needed |
| *possible* | a differential that cannot be excluded | cannot be excluded |
| *probable* | more likely than not | likely but not confirmed |
| *likely* | the most probable explanation | most probable |
| *suspected* | clinical suspicion sufficient to act | action warranted (e.g. "suspected sepsis") |
| *presumed* | assumed in absence of contrary evidence | assumed unless disproven |
| *working diagnosis* | current leading differential, subject to revision | explicitly revisable |
| *differential* / *DDx* | list of plausible alternatives | alternatives open |
| *not excluded* | differential still open | cannot be ruled out |
| *awaiting* | pending results | results pending |

The **"?"** prefix is the most common shorthand: *"?PE"*, *"?appendicitis"*, *"?sepsis"* — placed before a suspected diagnosis to mark it as provisional.

The term **"working diagnosis"** is endorsed by the RCP Edinburgh as a means to reduce cognitive bias — it signals that the diagnosis is the current leading differential, subject to revision with new evidence, and guards against anchoring and premature closure. Use it where uncertainty remains.

Do not over-hedge: if the diagnosis is confirmed by blood test, write *"confirmed"* — not *"possible"*. Do not under-hedge: if it is a clinical suspicion, write *"?* or *"suspected"* — not *"confirmed"*.

## Relevance — what to write and what to cut

**GMC Good Medical Practice (2024), paragraph 70** requires a **proportionate** approach to the level of detail. The record should usually include:

- a. relevant clinical findings
- b. drugs, investigations or treatments proposed, provided or prescribed
- c. the information shared with patients
- d. concerns or preferences expressed by the patient that might be relevant to their ongoing care, and whether these were addressed
- e. information about any reasonable adjustments and communication support preferences
- f. decisions made, actions agreed (including decisions to take no action) and when/whether decisions should be reviewed
- g. who is creating the record and when

Write what a colleague taking over the patient needs to know. Do not write what every entry in this patient's record already says, unless it has changed. Do not write what the patient's test results already state in a linked report — refer to them. Normal findings should be recorded when they have significance for diagnosis, continued investigation, and correct treatment.

## Phrasing patterns — SOAP in practice

**S — Subjective:** State what the patient reports, in their words where useful, plus absence of key symptoms. *"Headache since this morning, no fever or nausea."* Not a retelling of the full history.

**O — Objective:** Findings, not process. *"Abdomen soft, no palpable masses, no rebound. Liver not palpable."* *"Back: inspection unremarkable, palpation: no spasm, SLR negative bilaterally."* Record the score values: *"NEWS2 = 3", "GCS E4V5M6 = 15", "AMTS 10/10"*.

**A — Assessment:** Connect findings to conclusion. *"Breathlessness and swollen legs suggest heart failure. Differential: pulmonary embolism not excluded."* Use the working diagnosis where uncertainty remains.

**P — Plan:** State what will happen, who is responsible, and the timeframe. *"Starting furosemide 40 mg once daily. Check sodium and creatinine in 3 days. GP review in 1 week."* Include safety-netting (see below).

### SBAR in practice

**S:** "This is Dr [name], FY2 on [ward]. I'm calling about Mr [name], NHS number [number], a 72-year-old admitted with pneumonia. He has become acutely breathless in the last 30 minutes."

**B:** "Admitted 2 days ago with community-acquired pneumonia. PMH: heart failure. NEWS2 was 3 this morning, now 7."

**A:** "I think he is deteriorating — likely pulmonary oedema or sepsis. He is hypoxic (SpO2 88% on 2L) and tachycardic (HR 110)."

**R:** "I'd like you to review him urgently. Should I start IV furosemide and move him to a monitored bed?"

## Standard scores — document the values

| Score | What it is | When to use |
|---|---|---|
| **NEWS2** | National Early Warning Score (RCP 2017, NHS England endorsed) — 6 physiological parameters (resp rate, SpO2, systolic BP, pulse, consciousness/new confusion, temp) | All acutely ill adult inpatients. ≥5 = urgent review, "think sepsis"; ≥7 = critical care. Not for under-16s or pregnant women |
| **GCS** | Glasgow Coma Scale (Teasdale & Jennett 1974) — Eye/Verbal/Motor, 3–15 | Neurological observations. Document as components: E4V5M6 = 15. Mark untestable components "NT" |
| **AMTS** | Abbreviated Mental Test Score (Hodkinson 1972) — 10-point screen | Cognitive screen for delirium/dementia. ≤6 suggests impairment. Increasingly supplemented by 4AT for delirium |

## Safety-netting — document the specifics

**Safety-netting** (Neighbour, 1987; NICE definition) must be documented with specifics, not vague "come back if worse." Record:

1. **Specific symptoms to watch for** — the red flags
2. **Timeframe** for expected resolution or when to return
3. **How to seek further help** — which service to contact
4. **Explicit invitation to return** — even for the same symptoms
5. **What to do if symptoms persist or recur** — even if previous tests were negative ("three strikes and you are in" for cancer safety-netting)
6. **That the patient understood the advice**

Do not write *"safety-net advice given"* alone — state the symptoms, timeframe, and action. *"Advised to return immediately if breathlessness worsens, chest pain, or haemoptysis. Otherwise GP review in 48 hours. Patient understood."*

## Tone — professional, not personal

Write in the **third person** about the patient. The record is not a personal log — *"The patient"* not *"my patient"*.

Do not use evaluative or emotional language about the patient. *"Non-compliant"* is a clinical assessment when supported; *"difficult patient"* is a personal judgement with no clinical content. Describe the behaviour, not the person: *"The patient declines to take prescribed medication"* not *"the patient is non-compliant"* unless the pattern is established and documented.

Avoid stigmatising language. A patient who uses substances is not *"addict"* as an identity — describe the use: *"The patient uses heroin, last use yesterday."*

## Information and consent — the language of consent

Record what was said and whether consent was given. Not *"Patient given information and consented"* — that is a template. *"Patient given verbal information about the benefits and risks of treatment with metformin, including risk of lactic acidosis. Gives informed consent to treatment."*

The requirement to record information **increases with the complexity of treatment and the risk of complications**. Conversely, if the patient already knows the treatment and there are no changes to prior information, it suffices to record that the patient is informed and consents.

If the patient **declines** offered examination or treatment, record this and the information given about consequences. If the patient has asked **not to be told** information (the right not to know), note this. **Implied consent:** for routine elements where agreement is clear from the situation, consent does not need to be recorded directly — but the note must show it was a treatment situation covered by implied consent.

## Corrections — the language of amendment

Records **must not be deleted or made illegible**. Corrections are **additive**, not destructive:

*"Addendum 15/03: The above note refers to the left, not the right, knee. Correction made by [name, role]."*

In an electronic record, the original version must remain accessible after any edit. The correction is a new note, not an overwrite. Typographical errors may be corrected during the entry itself — but once the note is complete, only additive corrections.

## Critical findings and closed-loop communication

When communicating a critical or unexpected finding by telephone or in person, the documentation must capture both **what was said** and **that it was received**. UK practice uses **closed-loop communication** (AHRQ TeamSTEPPS, adopted in NHS):

1. **Call-out:** Sender initiates a clear message, directed to a named person
2. **Check-back (read-back):** Receiver repeats the message back
3. **Confirm:** Sender verifies the repeat-back was correct

Document: who was informed (name and grade), when (date and time), what was communicated (the result and its significance), the agreed action, and confirmation that the information was received. *"Critical potassium 6.8 mmol/L discussed by telephone with Dr [name], registrar, at 14:35. Result and need for urgent treatment communicated. Dr [name] confirmed understanding and will review immediately."*

There is no single named UK equivalent of the Danish VAF/VUF/VIF tiers — the combination of SBAR for communication, closed-loop for verification, and GMC-mandated documentation of who/when/what/action serves the same function.

## Duty of candour — when things go wrong

There is both a **statutory** duty of candour (Regulation 20, Health and Social Care Act 2008 Regulated Activities Regulations 2014 — a criminal offence if breached) and a **professional** duty (GMC/NMC joint guidance). When a notifiable safety incident occurs:

- Notify the patient (or relevant person) in person, as soon as reasonably practicable
- Give an account of the incident, to the best of your knowledge true of all facts known at the time
- Advise what further enquiries are appropriate
- **Offer an apology** (saying sorry is not an admission of liability)
- **Keep a written record** of the notification and all conversations and correspondence

For the full legal framework (GMP 2024 paras 69–71, Regulation 17/20, Caldicott Principles, GDPR, retention schedules), see [LEGISLATION.md](LEGISLATION.md).

## Prescribing — the language of prescriptions

A UK NHS prescription (FP10, or via the Electronic Prescription Service) is structured by tradition and law. The BNF "Prescription writing" guidance governs notation:

- **Drug name:** approved (non-proprietary) title, written in full, not abbreviated. Generic prescribing is the NHS default.
- **Form:** e.g. tablets, capsules, oral solution
- **Strength:** e.g. 125 mg/5 mL (liquid strengths must be clearly stated)
- **Quantity to be supplied:** number of days or exact amount
- **Dose and frequency:** e.g. "40 mg four times daily"
- **Route:** where appropriate

**Safe dose expression (BNF):**
- ≥ 1 g: write "1 g", "1.5 g"
- < 1 g: write in milligrams — "500 mg", not "0.5 g"
- < 1 mg: write in micrograms — "100 micrograms", not "0.1 mg"
- "micrograms" and "nanograms" must **not** be abbreviated
- "units" must **not** be abbreviated
- Use "mL", never "c.c." or "cm³"
- Avoid decimals where possible; if used, always include a leading zero (0.5 mL not .5 mL) and avoid trailing zeros (3 mg not 3.0 mg)
- For "as directed" / "when required", specify a **minimum dose interval**

**Brand prescribing required** for: ciclosporin, lithium, antiepileptics, MR preparations (morphine, diltiazem, nifedipine, methylphenidate), all biological/biosimilar medicines (insulins, monoclonal antibodies), adrenaline auto-injectors, and inhalers. Do not switch these.

**Controlled Drugs (Schedules 2–3)** have extra legal requirements: form stated, strength where more than one exists, quantity in **words and figures**, handwritten signature, dated, 28-day validity. See [PRESCRIBING.md](PRESCRIBING.md).

## Medication in the record

When recording a medication order, the record must contain: name, strength, quantity, dose, route, frequency, dosing times, duration, and who prescribed. The prescription must be in a **single system** — in hospital, the electronic medication ordering system, not the prose record.

For exact legal citation, the BNF prescription writing rules, controlled drug schedules, drug form abbreviations, charges and exemptions, and the "do not switch" list, see [PRESCRIBING.md](PRESCRIBING.md).

## NHS Number and patient identification

The **NHS Number** is the unique 10-digit identifier for patients in England, Wales, and the Isle of Man (format 3-3-4, with a modulus 11 check digit). It is required by the NHS Standard Contract for all clinical staff from 1 April 2020. Always include the NHS Number when identifying a patient in records shared between care settings — alongside name and date of birth. Do not rely on name alone.
